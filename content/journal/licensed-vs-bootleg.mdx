Bootlegs are not hard to spot once you know where to look. The problem is that almost nobody looks, because a marketplace listing is designed so you do not have to. The photo is sharp, the price is friendly, the reviews are fine. Here is what to check instead, in the order we check it.

## 1. Find the copyright line

Licensed merchandise carries a copyright notice naming the rights holder: a © symbol, a year, and the name of the studio, publisher, or production committee that owns the property. On apparel it is on the neck label or a hem tag. On desk goods and wall art it is printed on the packaging or the back of the piece. On a boxed item it is on the box, usually near the barcode.

A bootleg either omits it or gets it wrong. Wrong looks like a copyright line that names the *manufacturer* rather than the rights holder, a year that predates the series, or a line printed as an image so it can be swapped between products. If a seller cannot show you the copyright line in a photo when asked, treat the item as unlicensed.

## 2. Find the licensee

The copyright line tells you who owns the property. The licensee is who was granted the right to make this product, and a genuine item names them too: a brand name on the tag, the box, or the woven label. Search that brand. A real licensee has its own site with a product catalogue, and the item you are looking at appears in it. A bootleg's "brand" is a string of capital letters registered last year with no catalogue of its own.

This step catches the largest class of fakes: listings that steal a real licensee's product photos and ship something else.

## 3. Compare the price to the official one

Licensed products carry a royalty, typically somewhere between a few percent and the low teens of the wholesale price, plus approval costs and minimum production runs. That is why a licensed tee costs what it costs. A listing at a third of the licensee's own price is not a bargain; it is a different product. Real discounts appear on the licensee's or retailer's own site first.

## 4. Look at the print, not the photo

Product photos are often stolen, so they tell you little. Reviews with customer photos tell you more. Look for:

- **Colour banding** in gradients, and blacks that print as dark grey.
- **Soft edges** on line art that should be crisp: the sign of a low-resolution source image scaled up.
- **Mistranscribed text.** Bootleg factories copy text they cannot read, so kana and kanji come out subtly wrong and English gets letter-swapped.
- **Generic blanks.** A licensed tee is usually printed on a specific blank the licensee chose. A bootleg is on whatever was cheapest that week, so the fit and weight vary from one review to the next.

## 5. Check how many "designs" the shop sells

Open the seller's storefront. A licensee or a licensed retailer sells a bounded range: the products it holds licences for. A bootlegger sells everything — thousands of designs across dozens of unrelated properties, all added in the same week, all on the same three blanks. Breadth is the tell. Nobody has a licence for everything.

## 6. Read the euphemisms as what they are

"Inspired by." "Fan-made." "Unofficial." "Anime style." "Not affiliated with any brand." These phrases exist because platforms remove listings that claim to be official, so sellers stopped claiming. Each one is a statement that the seller does not hold a licence.

There is one honest exception, and it matters. An independent artist selling **their own original design**, with no character art and no franchise imagery, is not a bootlegger. They own the work. The [Panel Cut Heavyweight Tee](/desk/panel-cut-heavyweight-tee) and the [Neon Panel Desk Mat](/desk/neon-panel-desk-mat) in our catalog are labelled *Original design* for exactly this reason: nobody licensed them because there was nothing to license. What separates that from a bootleg is whether the design is genuinely theirs, and you can usually tell by looking at the rest of their shop.

## 7. Know who the seller actually is

On a marketplace, the storefront you are looking at and the business shipping your parcel are often different. Scroll to "sold by" and "ships from". A brand-direct store or a named licensed retailer is the strongest signal there is. A marketplace seller with a randomly generated name and a warehouse address is the weakest. It is why every pick on this site names the seller type before it names anything else.
